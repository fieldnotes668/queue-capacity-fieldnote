Why the queue can stay busy after traffic falls

A worker pool has a maximum throughput. If 160 jobs arrive each minute while the workers finish only 100, the queue grows by 60 jobs per minute. When arrivals drop back to 80, the workers have just 20 spare slots per minute for old work. A 600-job backlog then needs about 30 more minutes to drain.

next backlog = max(0, current backlog + arrivals − completed jobs)

The model uses that rule once per minute. It assumes a FIFO queue, no job failures, no priority classes, and a fixed service rate. Real queues vary; the point here is to show the capacity relationship, not predict an exact production wait time.

What the numbers mean

Peak waiting is the largest number of jobs left in the queue at the end of any simulated minute. At minute 60 shows whether the backlog has cleared within the chart window. Clear after burst estimates the drain time from the end of the burst at the normal arrival rate. When normal arrivals already meet or exceed capacity, it reports “Never” because this simple model has no spare capacity to catch up.

The estimated drain time is a capacity calculation, not a promise to an individual request. A FIFO job's wait also depends on where it entered the queue and how long actual jobs take.

Operational questions this model suggests

Before adding more workers, ask:

  • Is the work safe to run concurrently, or will extra workers overload a database or provider?
  • Can arrivals be slowed with admission control before the queue becomes unbounded?
  • How old is the oldest queued job? A small queue of slow jobs may be worse than a large queue of quick ones.
  • Are failures and retries adding new work faster than workers can complete it?

A production dashboard should show arrival rate, completion rate, oldest-job age, retry volume, and queue length together. Queue length alone cannot tell you whether the system is recovering.

Try two revealing cases

First, set normal arrivals just below capacity and make the burst large. The queue eventually drains, but slowly. Then raise normal arrivals to match or exceed capacity. The burst may end, yet the queue never catches up because no spare capacity remains. That difference is easy to miss when looking only at the current traffic rate.
